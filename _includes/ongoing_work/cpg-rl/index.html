① Why I looked into CPG-RL. A standard RL locomotion policy can learn effective behaviour, but its internal control structure is often difficult to interpret. In contrast, CPG-based formulations explicitly introduce phase, amplitude, and rhythmic coordination, which makes the generated motion easier to analyse in terms of periodic structure.

② The question that interested me. In CPG-RL, the oscillator itself has a stable limit-cycle structure, but it is less clear how much of the final robot-level robustness comes from this oscillator property, and how much comes from the downstream mapping, low-level control, and RL optimisation. This became the main question behind my reproduction and follow-up experiments.

The original CPG-RL [1] is not open-sourced. I reproduced the method from scratch in Isaac Lab by following the equations and training design described in the paper. The reproduced policy partially recovers the expected behaviour, although some gap remains, likely due to implementation details not fully specified in the paper, as well as simulator and training-pipeline differences.

One technical detail that stood out to me is that the oscillator states are not mapped directly to joint targets. Instead, they are first converted into Cartesian foot trajectories:

\[ x_{i,\mathrm{foot}} = -d_{\mathrm{step}}(r_i - 1)\cos(\theta_i)\cos(\phi_i) \] \[ y_{i,\mathrm{foot}} = -d_{\mathrm{step}}(r_i - 1)\cos(\theta_i)\sin(\phi_i) \] \[ z_{i,\mathrm{foot}} = \begin{cases} -h + g_c \sin(\theta_i), & \text{if } \sin(\theta_i) > 0 \\ -h + g_p \sin(\theta_i), & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

This mapping turns oscillator states into target foot positions, from which the final gait is generated. It led me to a practical question: in this formulation, is CPG mainly providing oscillator-level stability, or is it acting primarily as a structured periodic prior whose final effectiveness still depends heavily on the hand-designed mapping to robot motion?

To probe this question, I also tested a simpler joint-space baseline in which each joint is parameterised directly by a sinusoidal phase:

\[ q_{ij}(t) = b_{ij} + a_{ij}\sin\big(\psi_{ij}(t)\big) \]

Here, \(q_{ij}(t)\) is the target angle of joint \(j\) on leg \(i\), \(b_{ij}\) is the standing-posture bias, \(a_{ij}\) is the joint amplitude, and \(\psi_{ij}(t)\) is the RL-controlled phase. This baseline removes most explicit inter-joint structure while preserving a phase-based parameterisation, making it useful for testing how much structure is really needed.

In preliminary flat-terrain experiments, this baseline was already sufficient to produce running behaviour. I then varied the joint amplitude ratio \(a_{ij}\) from 20% to 100% of the joint range limit. Larger amplitudes consistently improved performance: at 20%, the robot failed after only a few steps, while at 100% it achieved the most stable behaviour in this setting, and selected tracking metrics such as error_velocity_xy and error_angular_velocity_z approached those of direct joint-target control. At this stage, I view this only as an initial result on flat terrain rather than a general conclusion.

Amplitude 20% — fails after a few steps

Amplitude 100% — most stable in my current setting

Based on my current reproduction results, preliminary experiments, and reading of the literature, my working hypothesis is that some CPG+RL formulations may use CPG mainly as a periodic and interpretable parametrisation, while the actual disturbance recovery of the full robot is still strongly shaped by the downstream mapping and RL-trained control policy. In other words, oscillator-level limit-cycle stability does not automatically translate into full robot-level locomotion stability.

What interests me most going forward is a stronger formulation in which phase is not only interpretable, but also dynamically coupled to locomotion recovery under perturbation. That would make phase a meaningful state variable of the motion itself, rather than merely a convenient periodic parameterisation.
